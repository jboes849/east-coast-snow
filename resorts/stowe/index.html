Method

Built for skiers
01

Eight Models, Weighted by Range

Every six hours we pull eight models at each summit and base: high-resolution HRRR, NAM and Canadian HRDPS, plus the NWS Blend, GFS, ECMWF, Canadian GEM and German ICON. The high-resolution models see the terrain best, so they carry the most weight for the first two days. Each model's weight also moves with its recent track record in that part of the mountains.

02

Real Odds From 82 Ensemble Runs

The GFS and European ensembles rerun their models dozens of times with small changes. We count how many runs reach 3, 6 or 12 inches, so "70% chance of 6 inches" means something.

03

Snow, Sleet or Rain

Snow, sleet or rain is decided by humidity-adjusted temperature at the summit and base plus the air a mile up, where warm layers turn snow into sleet. How fluffy the snow is comes from the warmest air the flakes fall through, and the wind.

04

Season Aware

Before opening day there are no ski grades. Instead we show when each mountain usually opens, the first natural snow, and nights cold enough to make snow in the last weeks before opening. Once lifts turn, every day gets an A–F grade for fresh snow, rain, refrozen surfaces, wind holds and cold.

Glossary
Range
The lowest and highest total among the weather models. A wide range means the forecast is uncertain.
Snow feel
How dense the new snow is. Cold air makes light, fluffy snow (an inch of water becomes 15+ inches of snow); air near freezing makes heavy, wet snow (under 8 inches).
Snow level
The elevation where rain turns to snow, roughly 1,000 ft below the freezing level.
Overnight snow
Snow that falls from 4pm to 9am, so it's waiting for you on the first chair.
Wind hold
When gusts get too strong (often above 45 mph) resorts stop upper lifts for safety.
Humidity-adjusted temp
Also called wet bulb: the air temperature adjusted for humidity. Dry air at 32°F can feel like the 20s to falling snow, which is why snow can fall at 36°F and why resorts can make snow above freezing.
Cold enough to make snow
Six or more hours with a mid-mountain humidity-adjusted temp at or below 26°F. Only shown in the weeks before a mountain usually opens. It's a sign the weather allows it, not that the resort will run its guns.
Ensemble
One model run dozens of times with slightly different starting conditions. The spread of results shows how sure the forecast is.
Sleet and freezing rain
Snow melts in a warm layer aloft, then refreezes into pellets (sleet) or freezes on contact with the cold ground (freezing rain).
Day grade
A quick A–F rating for skiing that day. A: fresh snow or a bluebird day. C: a normal groomer day. F: rain or lifts likely closed.
Nor'easter
A coastal storm tracking up the Atlantic with northeast winds. The classic big-dump setup for southern New England, the Catskills and Maine.
Lake effect
Narrow, intense snow bands off the Great Lakes on cold westerly winds. Huge totals for Tug Hill and western New York.
Upslope snow
Snow squeezed out as moist air is forced up the mountains. It's why the northern Greens and Adirondacks often beat the forecast.
